HOW TO CREATE A THESIS

  • The step in your research in which you read an encyclopedia article is very important in helping to develop a thesis.  Look closely at the article and try to separate the obvious facts (the day someone was born or what year he became President or the name and date of a battle that he won) from the subjective statements ("Caesar was ruthless."  or "Caesar wanted to become a king.").

  • One very useful way to develop a good thesis is to acquaint yourself with what professional historians argue about concerning your topic.  That is why the note-taking step should include background research concerning what historians say about the topic, after you have acquainted yourself with the topic in general.  For example, you may find that they argue about whether or not Caesar wanted to be king or whether he was an effective military leader.

  • Choose one aspect of the larger topic
    If you are writing about Julius Caesar, for example, you would not just write about the most important events in his life, but you may choose to write about his campaign in Gaul or his assassination.
     

  • Make an argument
    Using the example from Caesar and his campaign in Gaul, you might assert, as an argument, that Caesar's Gaul campaign was a significant factor that contributed to his popularity.
     

  • The thesis should be able to be proven


  • The thesis should have multiple parts


  • It should be a history paper
    The following is a good history thesis:  "The Anti-abortion movement in the United States achieved greater success after it stopped presenting abortion as a religious issue but as a rights issue".   The thesis does not depend on your own feelings about abortion (nor anyone else's for that matter), but makes an assertion about why an event happened the way it did.

Evidence for you thesis should exist.
The thesis that "The conspiracy to kill President Kennedy was so successful that evidence of the conspiracy has been destroyed" is not able to be proven, since the thesis actually states that ALL proof has been destroyed.
Another example of a logically impossible thesis is "Benito Mussolini's fascist ideas are an outgrowth of his unconscious desire to become more famous and successful than his father". If the desires are unconscious, then there cannot be evidence for these desires.
The thesis that "The signing of Jackie Robinson to play for the Brooklyn Dodgers was a major contributor to the American Civil Rights movement" is perfectly fine (though a bit over done).  But how do you PROVE your statement.  Just because one event precedes or follows another event does not mean that one event CAUSED the other.  Another example is the thesis that "President Roosevelt wanted the Japanese to attack the United States at Pearl Harbor so that he would support for American entry into World War Two".  The fact that the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor caused the United States to declare war on Japan does NOT PROVE the thesis.  What information would you NEED to have in order to prove either of these two statements?  That is the type of question that you need to ask as you proceed to research and write your paper.

You can create a multipart thesis by referring, in your thesis paragraph, to commonly-held views on the topic, the views held by historians on the topic, and the significance of the topic (for example: "Abraham Lincoln's administration during the Civil War is often pointed to as a model of Presidential leadership-- firm, yet restrained. In fact, the public, as well as ;rofessional historians, presently hold Lincoln in the highest esteem among all the Presidents of the United States. But Lincoln was not a strong and effective President. Rather, he was dominated by his cabinet and often deferred to them. His weakness was particularly evident during the events leading up to the appointment of a Commander in Chief of Union forces in the first year of the Civil War."). A multipart thesis allows you to effectively organize and develop your paper, as each section of the thesis becomes a section of your final paper, following the same order as your thesis paragraph.

  • A history paper is not a listing or narration of facts and events.
  • Extravagant statements and judgments should be avoided.
    For example, "Caesar was the greatest man who ever lived" is not something that can proven, since you are unlikely to have studied every person who ever lived AND your judgment is completely subjective.
  • A thesis is not a debate paper.  For example, "Abortion is murder" is a debate topic.  So is "abortion should be banned".
  • A thesis is not about "truth" or "lessons"
    "The Founding Fathers had the genius to create a Constitution that protects the Natural Rights that all human beings possess" is a poor thesis.  The idea that human beings possess natural rights is an opinion and NOT an ultimate truth.

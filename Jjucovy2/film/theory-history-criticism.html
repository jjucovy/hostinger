Film: Theory, History and Criticism

Additional Articles

Since its appearance toward the latter years of the nineteenth century, film and its offspring (television, computer-generated video, and video games) have had a greater impact on humanity than any other art form. Central to this course is the contention that the reason for the success of film (which I will use as a shorthand term for all animated graphic representations) is that it comes close to fulfilling the ultimate goal of art, which is to entertain the consumer with the closest possible approximation to reality. The very success that film has achieved in mimicking reality has inspired some troubling questions. Most salient is the recognition that the virtual reality that film aspires to achieve is, ultimately, nearly indistinguishable from reality itself. And, as scientists have increasingly pointed out, the reason that virtual reality is so difficult to distinguish from reality is that the human mind is nothing more than a virtual reality machine, turning the real world "out there" into images, smells and sounds "in here." One of the goals of this course is to examine the ways in which film tricks the mind into appearing "real."



Even from its inception, film makers were aware of the troublesome questions that were raised by film. Some sought to rigorously duplicate the real world, others toyed with the artistic possibilities of film to challenge the viewer to confront the illusions of reality that were generated by film, and still others turned to expressionist techniques to achieve an emotional reality. During the course of this year, you will be exposed to each of these responses. While you may tend to prefer one type of movie or another, they are all equally important to and valid forms of film art. To a certain extent, these differing approaches reflect cultural variations among nations, and, aside from viewing important American films, you will be exposed to foreign cinema, which has tended to be more experimental, more "artistic."

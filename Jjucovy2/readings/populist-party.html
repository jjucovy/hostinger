Populist Party

GILDED AGE POLITICS and SOCIETY:

THE PEOPLE's PARTY

The rise of the Populist Movement in the 1890's was a result of the crisis in American agriculture. Farmers had borrowed large sums of money during and after the Civil War to meet the growing demand for their produce. As more crops were produced for the new national market, farm prices fell and it became more difficult to pay back the borrowed sums. Additionally, the increasing mechanization of farming increased farm costs, as did the need to ship produce over the railroads to the distant markets. Even though seed, fertilizer, machinery, storage and rail costs probably declined, they did not decline as rapidly as farm income. And the dependence on distant manufacturers and railroad owners, not to mention bankers, created a mood of helplessness and desperation among farmers. Farmers agitated in their states, notably the Midwest, for laws to protect them from the "monopolists." At first, the Supreme Court, in Munn v. Illinois permitted state regulation of storage fees paid to railroads. But in the Wabash Case, the Court ruled that states could not regulate businesses engaged in interstate commerce, like railroads. The stage was now set for farmers to move from local politics (through their Granger organizations) to the formation of a national party, the Populist Party.
The Populists major regional organizations were the Southern Alliance and the Northwestern Alliance. There was also an African-American Populist organization, the Colored Farmers' National Alliance, that cooperated with the Southern Alliance. The major differences between the Southern and Northwestern Alliance centered directly and indirectly on the issue of African-Americans. The Southern Alliance emphasized secrecy, separation of the races, and working through the Democratic Party (in order not to divide the white vote and undermine white supremacy). The Northwestern Alliance was more interested in forming a new third party. By 1890, a first step was taken with the adoption of the Ocala Demands
The People's (or Populist) Party was finally organized in 1892 and ran candidates in that election for President and locally. Even the Southern Alliance finally agreed to cooperate with a third party movement. The new party received more than a million votes for its Presidential candidate, James Weaver, and elected numerous congressmen, Senators, and governors in the Mid-West. The results in the South were more disappointing, as Democrats used repressive tactics to keep their control. In fact, the adoption of Jim Crow laws, including the denial of voting rights to African-Americans, may have occurred because the white Southern establishment was afraid that the Populists might threaten white supremacy. By stirring up racial hatred, "divide and conquer," cooperation between whites and African-Americans was crushed. The Populists also fared poorly in urban centers, as immigrants found little reason to be attracted to, and much to be suspicious of, in the Populist Movement. The elections 1892 was the highpoint of the Populists. And the Party sealed its doom when they agreed to support the Presidential candidacy of the Democratic Party nominee in 1896, William Jennings Bryan, in his race against the Republican candidate, William McKinley.
The election of 1896 was a watershed election. Running on a platform of "Free Silver," Bryan stirred millions with his speeches, of which the "Cross of Gold" speech at the Democratic nominating convention was the most famous. The silver issue was regarded by its supporters with religious-like faith and by its opponents with equally emotional fear. A pamphlet that supported free silver, Coin's Financial School, written by William "Coin" Harvey, was one of the greatest best-sellers in American history. Nonetheless, Bryan was soundly defeated by McKinley, giving the Republican Party firm control of the national government, with few exceptions, until the Great Depression and the New Deal of the 1930's. The Populist were finished. Americans now seemed prepared to accept, to a large degree, the new realities of urbanization and industry.

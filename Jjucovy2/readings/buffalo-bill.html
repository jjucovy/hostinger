Buffalo Bill Road Show

As the frontier was being "closed," Americans became even more fascinated with the way of life, the "exotic" populations, and the life forms that were rapidly disappearing. The popularity of Buffalo Bill and his Wild West Show was one example of this interest. Buffalo Bill was a part of a long-standing cultural tradition in America. The frontier hero was a common figure in the American imagination. Some of our heroes were fictional, like Paul Bunyan. Some were semi-fictional, like Johnny Appleseed. Some were real, like Davey Crockett. But they all testify to the importance of the frontier to Americans and, even, Europeans still living in the "old world." William Cody (Buffalo Bill) was a combination of fact and fiction. The original Buffalo Bill was the hero of novels written by Prentiss Ingraham. But William Cody soon adopted the role, playing Buffalo Bill to the delight of Eastern audiences in his Buffalo Road Show. Thus, the hero of a novel, Buffalo Bill, was depicted in a road show, played by William Cody, who enacted, in real life, the deeds of Buffalo Bill.  Featured acts included Indian Races (with horses and buffalo, cowboy deeds of daring, Annie Oakley, and Civil War re-enactments.One of the greatest triumphs for Buffalo Bill came at the Chicago World's Fair in 1893. World's Fairs were a highly popular form of mass entertainment in the late nineteenth century, the equivalent of the modern day theme parks. Like the theme parks of today, the World Fairs combined entertainment, thrills, and "education."
They were arenas where manufacturers sought to promote products, where states and provinces competed for new residents and new investments, where urban spaces were organized into shimmering utopian cities, and where people from all social classes went to be alternately amused, instructed, and diverted from more pressing concerns. Memorialized in songs, books, buildings, public statuary, city parks, urban designs, and photographs, fairs were intended to frame the world view not only of the hundreds of millions who attended these spectacles, but also the countless millions who encountered the fairs secondhand. The Columbian Exposition was very much a part of this tradition. It attempted to redefine America for itself and the world, and in doing so introduced many themes and artifacts still prevalent in American life: the connection between technology and progress; the predominance of corporations and the professional class in the power structure of the country; the triumph of the consumer culture; and the equation of European forms with "high culture", as well as the more pedestrian legacy of Juicy Fruit Gum, Pabst Blue Ribbon beer, ragtime music, and Quaker Oats.
(Julie K. Rose, http://xroads.virginia.edu/~MA96/WCE/introduction.html )

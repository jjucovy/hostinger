Gilded Age Politics 2

GILDED AGE POLITICS and SOCIETY

 

In 1890, the Republicans swept into control of both houses of Congress and already controlled the Presidency (Benjamin Harrison). The new Congress (labeled the "Billion Dollar Congress" by Democrats for its spending programs) swiftly enacted or tried to enact a number of major laws, including a higher tariff (the McKinley Tariff), a pension for Civil War veterans of the Union Army, the Sherman Anti-Trust Act, and protections for African-Americans voting rights (which the Senate rejected). A combination of voter disapproval of the high tariff, "excessive" government spending and intrusion (Republicans in Midwestern states had passed Blue Laws and required the use of English in all schools), and, perhaps, racism led to a huge defeat for the Republicans in 1892, bringing Cleveland back into the Presidency and Democratic control of the House and Senate. But Cleveland's victory was short-lived, as the Depression of 1893 erupted.

The Depression that began in 1893 caused widespread and unprecedented misery in the United States. Unemployment rose to almost 20% of the workforce and remained above 10% for half a decade. One of the most important consequences of the Depression, which ended by 1897, was the growing public acceptance of a government role to alleviate poverty and regulate the economy. But Cleveland resisted the calls for help. For example, Coxy's Army, which, led by Jacob Coxy, marched on Washington to demand government-sponsored jobs for road building, ended in Coxy's arrest. A railroad strike, the Pullman Strike, was suppressed by Federal Troops (which the Supreme Court approved of in In Re Debs). It was bad enough for Cleveland's reputation that he was deemed insensitive to poverty, but his measures to end the Depression, such as adopting the Gold Standard (by repealing the Sherman Silver Purchase Act) and borrowing money from J. P. Morgan and other bankers, appeared to be favoring the wealthy and powerful (even if they may have actually helped ease the economic crisis). The Democrats suffered huge losses in the elections of 1894. But the Republicans were not the only winners; the third party Populist Party all made many gains in the West and the South.

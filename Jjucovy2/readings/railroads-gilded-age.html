Railroads in Gilded Age America

The Republican Party, which generally controlled the national government from 1861 to 1915, was an active supporter of railroad construction. Its 1860 Party Platform called for the construction of a transcontinental railroad. After the Civil War, the project was begun, and it was completed in 1869 when the Union Pacific Railroad and the Central Pacific Railroad lines met at Promontory, Utah. Other lines, as well as trunk lines for local traffic were also built during this period (look at the maps and charts in chapter 18 on pages 522 to 525). Railroad construction was an expensive and risky business. The federal, state, and local governments provided some money, granted millions of acres of land, but most money was raised from investors, American and European. Obviously, the enormous expense would have to be recovered. Railroad companies, therefore, set up offices throughout America and Europe to advertise for settlers. In the nineteenth century, the United States, like many other areas of the world, did not have any standard measurement of the time of day. Every community was free to set its time as it saw fit, leading to many complications and much confusion, as the Instructions to Railroad Travellers (found midway down the page on this link) demonstrate (scroll down to the Instructions). Imagine arriving in Chicago at 2 P.M. on one railroad line and finding that train you were trying to catch had left fifteen minutes earlier at 2:30 P.M. on the time standard followed by another railroad line! Given the enormous distances and the movement of people and products over these distances, the railroad companies, borrowing the idea from English railroad companies, created standard time zones in 1883, so that passengers and businessmen could make sense out of the schedules. But the United States did not officially adopt standard zones until 1918.

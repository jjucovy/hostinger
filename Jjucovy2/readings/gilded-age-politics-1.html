Gilded Age Politics 1

The 1890's was a tumultuous decade in the United States. Huge industrial fortunes aroused suspicion and envy. The Depression of 1893 poisoned a decade. Agriculture was in crisis for part of this period. Workers engaged in frequent strikes which were accompanied by violence initiated by both employers and employees. Radical political parties, with socialist and anarchist goals, arose. Naturally, the political system which had gone largely unchallenged since the end of Reconstruction, was affected by these upheavals. Ultimately, the two party system weathered the storm, but the election of 1896 heralded new political alignments that left the Republican Party, once again, the dominant party in the nation until the Great Depression of the 1930's.

Among the notable features of the political system in the Gilded Age was the relative weakness of the national Executive Branch, ever since the impeachment of Andrew Johnson. In the aftermath of the Civil War, many people were also tired of "big government" and looked to the state and local governments to solve most problems. During the Gilded Age, voter turnout was very high, though very few African-Americans and women could vote (upheld by the Supreme Court in Williams v. Mississippi and Minor v. Happersett respectively. At the beginning of the 1890's, Democrats and Republicans maintained a near balance in national politics. The Republicans could usually count on winning the Presidency (only Grover Cleveland, in 1884 and 1892 would break the Republican monopoly). But in regional and local elections, reflected in the Senate and House of Representatives, the Democrats, because of the "Solid South," the support of those everywhere who opposed "big government," and the support of immigrants in urban centers in the Northeast, could hold their own. Democrats controlled the House of Representatives for sixteen years between 1874 and 1894. Even Presidential elections were usually so close that neither party dared to antagonize potential voters by advocating major policy changes. Instead, parties appealed to voters by nominating Presidential and Vice-Presidential candidates from "swing states" in the Northeast (states that did not lean toward either of the two parties) or by emotional appeals to voters. Republicans "waved the bloody shirt" by reminding voters that the Rebellion had been started by Democrats. Democrats accused the Republicans of promoting policies that favored the African-Americans. Both parties out-competed each other with hostile words or actions against England.

GILDED AGE POLITICS and SOCIETY

Two issues, in particular, agitated politicians, if not voters, during the Gilded Age: political corruption and the tariff question. Both parties were rife with corrupt officials, but since the Republicans usually controlled the national government, their corruption was more nationally prominent. In the Republican Party, agroup of reformers, the Mugwumps, were opposed by defenders of the status-quo, the Stalwarts. Those in-between were called Half-Breeds. When John Garfield was assassinated in 1881 by a disappointed (and probably disturbed) office-seeker, the new President, Chester Arthur, got Congress to pass a civil service reform law, the Pendleton Act, in 1881, even though Arthur was a member of the Stalwarts. The law required that a Civil Service Exam be administered in order to be considered for a small number of Federal jobs. On the tariff issue, Democrats generally opposed high tariffs, since they raised costs for farmers and workers. Republicans generally supported tariff "protection" for the industrialists who were major financial contributors to the party. But one should not assume that all Republicans, Democrats, farmers, workers, or businessmen were of one mind about the tariff.
